Physical Internet® · Land Use

We paved our cities for cars. We can get them back.

Land is the one thing a city cannot make more of. The car-centric road system spends it on moving and storing 2-ton vehicles. The Physical Internet® moves people and goods overhead on slim piers and gives the street back to people.

The Same Scale

One freeway interchange. One historic city center.

We Paved the Neighborhoods

Urban freeways were routed through the densest, poorest and often Black neighborhoods, the places where people lived, worked and shopped on foot. The Bragdon Committee (1957–1959) warned against it and recommended a moratorium on urban Interstates. The warning was ignored.

Parking Ate Downtown

Every car needs a space at home, at work and at every store. Parking minimums wrote that into law. Downtowns that once paid the city's bills became lots that pay almost nothing.

What that costs a city in tax revenue, per acre, is on the Metrics page.

Space to Move People

The problem is not the fuel. It is the packet size: a 2-ton vehicle carrying one person. Electric, hydrogen and self-driving cars all take the same road and the same parking. A JPods pod carries up to 500 kg overhead, and the street below stays open.

Traffic Breaks Neighborhoods

8× the traffic. 70% fewer friends.

Donald Appleyard studied three San Francisco streets. Residents of a street with 2,000 vehicles a day had 3.0 friends on the street. Residents of a street with 16,000 vehicles a day had 0.9. Heavy traffic cuts a neighborhood in half and people stop knowing each other.

Donald Appleyard, Livable Streets (University of California Press, 1981).

Mesh, Not Hub

Taking land back from cars only works if people can still get where they are going. A hub-and-spoke system forces trips through the center. A mesh connects every station to its neighbors, so the last mile shrinks to a short walk or bike ride.

Network value increases exponentially as the number of interconnected nodes increases and the packet size is driven toward one.
